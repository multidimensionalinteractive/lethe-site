ON WAR, MEMORY, AND ARTISTIC COMPULSION

A conversation about the photograph that reoriented the work, a return to painting, the relationship between personal history and historical encirclement, and why the Eastern Front became necessary as both artist and researcher.

I. The Photograph

The photograph that reoriented everything arrived by accident, the way most things that matter do. I was not looking for it. I was deep in research — the kind of hours-long archival wandering that had become my natural state — and there it was: a German prisoner photographed at Stalingrad.

He is standing, or being held upright. The coat is collapsing around him. His face is not a face in defeat the way we are taught to see defeat. It is something else. It is a man at the absolute end of a thing, and something in him is still refusing to perform for the camera.

I could not stop looking at him. And the more I looked, the more I understood that the looking itself was the subject — that something in me had been waiting for this exact encounter without knowing it.

The full account of that photograph — where it came from, what I know and do not know about the man in it — is the hinge of this conversation, and it stays with me every day I work.

II. The Return

There was a long period when I was not painting at all. A decade, in pieces. The work had stopped for reasons that had nothing to do with the work itself — personal history, the weight of things I had not yet found the language for, a life that needed to be lived before the paintings could be made.

It turned me from an observer into a witness. That is the only way I can say it. Before, I watched. After, I was accountable to what I had seen.

When I came back to the studio, it was not a return to an old practice. It was the beginning of a different one. The photograph had lit something. It was like a fuse was lit — and it has been burning since.

III. Encirclement

The historical encirclement — Stalingrad, the front, the enormous event holding individual lives inside it — became the parallel for an interior one. I have been held inside my own encirclements. Every person who has lived any kind of extremity knows the shape of that holding.

That is why the work is not a history lesson. It is not an illustration. It is an inside journey through how I perceive, in an intuitive sense, the world of the Eastern Front — and why that world will not let me go.

I do not invent biographies for the people I paint. I often do not know their names, whether they lived or died. I hold them in visibility without claiming knowledge I do not possess. The tension between remembrance and obliteration — Lethe — is the water I work in.

IV. The Necessity

Why share this now? Because the work has reached a scale and a clarity that can no longer stay in the studio. Because the material — the individuals, the knowledge, the value I believe it holds for viewers — deserves to be seen through the lens I see it through.

I know I have something. I want to show and share it with as many people as possible.

The front did not end. It became interior. And I am still inside it — painting my way toward the light that remains when the layers are removed.

ALEXANDRIA CHANEL — LETHE
